Instructions

Move with the arrow keys and drop a bomb with Z. Bombs explode in a cross shape after a few seconds, destroying soft blocks and anything standing in the blast - including you. The entire game is the tension between placing a bomb where it will do damage and being certain you have an escape route before you place it.

Blow up soft blocks to reveal power-ups. Fire extends your blast radius, extra bombs let you place more at once, and Speed makes you faster - though enough Speed pickups will make you genuinely hard to control, which is a real way to lose. The Remote Control lets you detonate on your own timing with X, and it is the strongest item in the game.

Think in lines, not squares. A bomb's blast travels along the row and column it sits in, so safety means standing behind a hard block or around a corner, never simply far away. The most common death in Bomberman is walking into a corridor that a bomb you had forgotten about is pointing straight down.

Controls

  • Arrow keys — move through the maze
  • Z — drop a bomb
  • X — detonate remotely, once you have the remote control power-up
  • Enter — START - begin and pause
  • V — SELECT - menus

Tips to Master Super Bomberman

Up to four players fight in a single screen arena until one is left, and with the SNES Multitap that was a genuine four-player party game in 1993. Matches last under a minute, the skill curve is shallow enough that anyone can join and deep enough that good players dominate, and the losing players become spectators who can still lob bombs in from the edge in some rulesets.

Trapping, not chasing

New players chase opponents around the maze. Experienced ones close off escape routes. Because the arena is a grid and blasts run in straight lines, the winning move is almost always to place a bomb that cuts off the corridor your opponent would retreat into, then place a second that covers where they actually are. Chasing tends to walk you into your own blast radius.

The single-player campaign

Normal Game runs through themed worlds of rooms, each requiring you to destroy every enemy before a door opens, with a boss at the end of each set. It supports two-player co-op, which changes it considerably - friendly fire is on, so a careless partner is at least as dangerous as the enemies. Progress is kept with passwords from the title screen rather than a save battery.

Why the formula never needed changing

Bomberman has barely altered since the 1980s because the core is close to perfect: one action, a hard timer on it, and a grid that makes every consequence legible at a glance. Nothing in a match is hidden from you, so every death is unambiguously your own error - which is exactly the quality that makes a multiplayer game worth replaying immediately.
