Instructions

Swim in any direction with the arrow keys, hold Z to accelerate and press X to charge into something. S is your sonar, and you will use it constantly: a short pulse maps the geometry around you and reveals passages, while aiming it at other sea creatures is how you talk to them and get told where to go next.

Watch the air meter. Ecco is a mammal and drowns, so the bar at the top of the screen is a constant background clock - surface to refill it, and plan any long underwater passage around where the next air pocket is. Running out is the most common way to die in the early game, and the level design is quite deliberately built to make you nervous about it.

Learn what to charge and what to avoid. Ecco's attack requires speed, so you must build up momentum before hitting something - tapping the button next to an enemy does nothing. Many hazards, particularly jellyfish and the spiky creatures, cannot be attacked at all and simply have to be swum around. Glyphs block your path until you sing at them, and some need a key glyph found elsewhere in the level.

Tips to Master Ecco the Dolphin

Most action games give you a weapon and build everything around it. Ecco gives you a voice. Sonar maps the terrain, opens the glyph barriers that gate each level, and is how every conversation in the game happens - whales, other dolphins and the odd stranger all respond to being sung at. It makes exploration feel genuinely different from swimming through a level with a map, because the world only reveals itself in the pulses you send out. That one design decision is why the game is still discussed thirty years on.

Much harder than it looks

The art and the subject matter suggest something gentle, and the game is not. Ecco is fragile, the air meter never stops draining, the levels are large mazes with easy-to-miss exits, and several later stages are genuinely brutal - the Welcome to the Machine section in particular has a long-standing reputation as one of the hardest sequences on the Genesis. Going in expecting a relaxing swim is the single most common reason people bounce off it.

A story that goes somewhere unexpected

What begins as a search for a missing pod becomes, over the course of the game, something involving time travel, an ancient undersea civilisation and an alien species that farms the ocean. Designer Ed Annunziata drew on fringe ideas about dolphin intelligence and lost civilisations that were circulating at the time, and the result is a game whose plot escalates in a direction essentially no player predicts from the first level. It is worth experiencing unspoiled.

The name

The dolphin is named for the echo in echolocation, and the game's Japanese release used a spelling reflecting that. The five-dot pattern on Ecco's head is a recurring motif, and the common claim that the game is named after oceanographer Jacques Cousteau or the Eco movement is not accurate - though the game's environmental sympathies are real enough, and were unusual for a mainstream console release in 1992.
