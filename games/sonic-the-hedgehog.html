Instructions

Run with the left and right arrows and jump with X or Z. Hold down while moving to curl into a roll, which lets you carry speed through low gaps and damages enemies on contact. Note that there is no spin dash in this first game - that was added in Sonic 2, so building speed here means using slopes and running room.

Rings are your health. Getting hit while holding at least one ring scatters them instead of killing you, and you can grab some back before they vanish. Getting hit with zero rings costs a life. That single rule is why collecting rings matters even when you are not chasing a score - one ring is the difference between a mistake and a death.

Fifty rings at the end of an act opens the Special Stage. Touch the giant ring after the signpost and you enter a rotating maze for a Chaos Emerald; collecting all six is the only way to see the good ending. The stages are disorienting by design, so treat the first few attempts as learning how the rotation works.

Controls

  • Down arrow — crouch, and roll while moving
  • X or Z — jump
  • Down + jump — spin dash is not in this game - hold down and roll instead

Tips to Master Sonic the Hedgehog

The common misreading of Sonic is that you hold right and go fast. In practice the game gives you speed when you have earned it with a clean line - the loops and slopes convert momentum you already had. Running blindly into a spike pit or a crab is how most first runs end. The design wants you to learn a zone, then run it fast, which is much closer to a racing game than to Mario.

Six zones, three acts each

Green Hill, Marble, Spring Yard, Labyrinth, Star Light and Scrap Brain each run three acts with a Robotnik fight at the end of the third. The pacing deliberately varies - Marble and Labyrinth slow you down almost completely, which players either read as a welcome change of rhythm or as the worst parts of the game. Labyrinth's drowning timer in particular is the most complained-about sequence in the series.

The drowning music

Staying underwater too long starts a countdown accompanied by a piece of music that a generation of players can still hum from memory. Air bubbles released from vents refill the timer, and the mechanic is genuinely tense because the music tells you exactly how much time you have left without a number on screen - a very neat piece of design that happens to be traumatic.

Why Sega needed this game

The Genesis was competing against a Nintendo console with Mario on it, and Sega needed a mascot and a technical showcase in one product. Sonic delivered both: an attitude-forward character to put on packaging, and a demonstration that the hardware could move large sprites across scrolling backgrounds faster than the competition. The console's fortunes in the West turned on it.
