Instructions

Drag a piece to the square you want it on. Illegal moves snap back, so you cannot accidentally play something the rules do not allow, and the board handles castling, en passant and promotion for you. The computer replies automatically once your move is accepted.

Set the search depth before you start. A low depth makes the engine fast and beatable; raising it makes it substantially stronger and noticeably slower, because the number of positions it has to examine grows enormously with each extra ply. Depth is effectively the difficulty slider.

Watch the positions-evaluated counter. It is the clearest illustration you will get of why chess is hard for computers: adding one move of lookahead multiplies the work rather than adding to it, which is exactly why real engines spend most of their effort on pruning branches rather than on searching faster.

Tips to Master Chess

The engine builds a tree of possible moves and replies, assumes you will always pick the move that is worst for it, and picks the move whose worst outcome is best. That is minimax. Each additional level of lookahead is called a ply, and because every ply multiplies the branches, the search grows exponentially - which is why the depth setting changes response time so dramatically.

Opening principles beat memorised lines

Against an engine at modest depth you do not need an opening repertoire. Control the centre with pawns, develop your knights and bishops before moving the same piece twice, castle early to get your king off the open file, and connect your rooks. Those four habits will produce a comfortable position against almost any shallow search.

How a shallow engine loses

An engine searching only a few ply cannot see a plan that pays off later, so it tends to grab material and accept structural weaknesses to do it. The practical exploit is to set traps slightly deeper than its horizon - offer a pawn that costs it the ability to castle, or trade into an endgame where a passed pawn decides things ten moves from now.

Endgames are where games are decided

Most players at club level lose winning positions in the endgame rather than the middlegame. Two ideas carry most of the weight: push passed pawns, and activate your king once the queens come off - in the endgame the king is a strong attacking piece rather than something to hide. An engine at low depth handles endgames particularly poorly, which makes them a reliable place to convert an advantage.
