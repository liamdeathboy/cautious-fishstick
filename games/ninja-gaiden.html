Instructions

Run with the arrow keys, jump with Z and swing the Dragon Sword with X. The wall cling is the mechanic everything else is built on: jump into a wall and Ryu grabs it, then press jump again to leap off. Climbing a shaft means alternating walls, and a great deal of the level design assumes you are comfortable doing it at speed.

Collect the red and blue scrolls for sub-weapons and spirit power. Hold up and press attack to use whatever art you have equipped - the Windmill Throwing Star is the one to keep, since it returns to you like a boomerang and costs relatively little. Press Enter to cycle between what you are carrying.

Learn where enemies respawn. Ninja Gaiden regenerates an enemy the moment its spawn point scrolls back on screen, which means backing up to line up a jump can create the bird or jumping ninja that knocks you into a pit. Moving forward decisively is safer than being cautious, which is the opposite of most platformer instincts.

Controls

  • Arrow keys — run, crouch and climb; press up at a wall to cling
  • Z — jump - press again on a wall to wall-jump
  • X — attack with the Dragon Sword
  • Up + X — use the equipped ninja art
  • Enter — START - pause and cycle sub-weapons

Tips to Master Ninja Gaiden

Tecmo called them cinema displays, and they bookend each of the six acts with camera angles, framing and dialogue that look like storyboards. In 1989 that was close to unprecedented on the NES, and it is the direct ancestor of a lot of what console action games did in the following decade. The opening duel under the moon is still the image most people picture when the game is mentioned.

Act 6, and the reason people rage about this game

The final act is where Ninja Gaiden's reputation comes from. Dying at any point during the three-part final boss sequence sends you back to the start of 6-1, not to a checkpoint, so a failed attempt costs you the entire act. Combined with aggressive knockback and pits, it produces the specific kind of difficulty that people remember decades later - not hard moments, but expensive ones.

Knockback is the real enemy

Taking a hit while airborne sends Ryu backwards, and most deaths in this game are bottomless pits reached that way rather than health running out. The practical consequence is that positioning matters more than damage: jumping over a gap with an enemy on screen is a gamble, and clearing threats before you commit to a jump is nearly always worth the extra seconds.

Speed as the core appeal

Beneath the difficulty, Ninja Gaiden is exhilarating because Ryu moves fast and responds instantly. There is no acceleration curve to fight, the sword swings the frame you press the button, and the wall jump lets a confident player cross a level in a continuous flow. Getting good at it feels less like memorising and more like learning to move, which is why it still holds up.
