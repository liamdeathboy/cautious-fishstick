Instructions

The left and right arrows work the flippers - each side independently, exactly as on a real table. Pull the plunger with down to launch. When Sonic comes to rest on a platform you can walk him around with the arrows and jump with X or Z, which is how you reach switches that the ball physics alone will not get you to.

Collect the Chaos Emeralds hidden in each table. Every stage has several, usually locked behind a sequence of bumpers or switches you need to trigger in order, and collecting them all opens the exit to the next table. The game will not tell you where they are - exploring the table's dead ends is the actual objective.

Learn the table before chasing points. Each of the four stages is a fixed structure with specific routes upward, and knowing which flipper shot sends you to which level is worth far more than reacting quickly. Losing the ball down the drain sends you back down through the table, so progress is measured in how reliably you can climb.

Controls

  • Left / Right arrows — operate the flippers on each side
  • Down arrow — pull back and release the plunger
  • X or Z — jump when Sonic is walking, and nudge the table
  • Enter — START - begin and pause

Tips to Master Sonic Spinball

The hybrid is the whole idea. Pure pinball would not need Sonic; a pure platformer would not need flippers. By letting you walk when you land and roll when you launch, the game makes the table into a level you explore rather than a scoreboard you feed. It does not fully succeed - the walking sections are stiff - but it is a genuinely unusual design rather than a cash-in.

Four tables, each a fortress floor

Toxic Caves, Lava Powerhouse, The Machine and Showdown escalate in size and complexity, and each has its own hazards - toxic sludge that rises, lava, crushing machinery. The later tables are large enough that you can lose track of where the exit is entirely, which is where a lot of players stop. The difficulty is less about reflexes than about map knowledge.

Made fast, and it shows

Sonic Spinball was developed on a compressed schedule to fill a gap in Sega's release calendar, and the results are uneven: the physics are heavier than the mainline games, the animation is rougher, and the difficulty was not smoothed out. It is still worth playing for the concept, but going in expecting the polish of Sonic 2 will lead to disappointment.

The soundtrack is a highlight

Howard Drossin's score is widely considered the best thing about the game - heavier and more rock-influenced than the mainline Sonic music, and a good demonstration of what the Genesis FM chip could do in the right hands. Toxic Caves in particular gets cited regularly in discussions of Genesis music.
