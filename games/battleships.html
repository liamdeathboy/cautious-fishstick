Instructions

Place your fleet first. Drag each ship onto the grid and rotate it to fit; the computer cannot see where they are, and neither can you see its arrangement. Once both sides have deployed, you alternate shots at single squares and are told only whether each one hit or missed.

Spread your ships out rather than clustering them. A player who hits one ship will search the squares around it, so a neighbouring vessel is likely to be found by accident. Leaving at least one empty square between hulls costs you nothing and makes each of your ships a separate search problem for the opponent.

When you get a hit, work along the axis. A hit tells you a ship occupies that square but not which way it lies, so fire directly above, below, left or right until you get a second hit - then keep going in that direction. Firing randomly after a hit throws away the only real information the game ever gives you.

Tips to Master Battleships: General Quarters

Because the smallest ship covers two squares, you never need to fire at every square to find something. Shooting in a checkerboard pattern - only the black squares, say - guarantees you will eventually touch every possible ship position while firing at half as many squares. It is the single biggest improvement most players can make and it costs nothing to adopt.

Hunt mode and target mode

Strong play alternates between two states. In hunt mode you are searching efficiently with a spread pattern; the moment you land a hit you switch to target mode and work the adjacent squares until the ship sinks, then return to hunting. Mixing the two - taking random shots while a wounded ship is still afloat - is the most common way players lose games they were winning.

Where the game came from

Battleship began as a pencil-and-paper game played in the early twentieth century, long before Milton Bradley published the plastic version in 1967. The Flash era gave it a second life on portals like Miniclip, where a computer opponent removed the need for a second person and a screen removed the need for anyone to peek.

Reading the computer's pattern

AI opponents in games of this era generally hunt randomly until they hit, then switch to adjacent search. That is exploitable: placing a ship hard against an edge or in a corner reduces the number of adjacent squares an opponent can search from a hit, which buys you turns. It is a slight edge rather than a guarantee, but the margins in this game are thin.
